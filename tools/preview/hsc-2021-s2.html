#1numeric-entryfrom Q21ainquiry 6.4
A DC motor has a single rectangular loop of wire measuring 0.10 m by 0.07 m, carrying a current of 14 A in a magnetic field of 0.40 T. Calculate the magnitude of the maximum torque produced.
0.0392N m
accepted within ±3% (relative)
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): $\tau_{max} = nBIA$ with $n = 1$ and $A = 0.10 \times 0.07$ m$^2$.
#2multiple-choicefrom Q21binquiry 6.4
Starting from the position where its plane is parallel to the field, how does the torque on the motor loop change as it turns through half a revolution?
  • ✓ It starts at a maximum, falls to zero after a quarter turn (plane perpendicular to the field), then rises again to a maximum (but tending to turn the loop the other way) after a half turn
  • It stays constant throughout the half revolution
  • It rises steadily from zero to a maximum over the half revolution
  • It falls steadily from a maximum to zero and stays at zero
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): $\tau = nBIA\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is measured between the coil's plane and the field; a commutator is needed to keep the motor turning the same way past the half-turn point.
#3multiple-choicefrom Q22inquiry 5.2
A horizontal disc rotates at a constant rate. Point X is twice as far from the centre as point Y. How do the angular and linear (instantaneous) velocities of X compare with those of Y?
  • ✓ X and Y have the same angular velocity, but X's linear speed is twice that of Y
  • X has twice the angular velocity and twice the linear speed of Y
  • X and Y have the same angular velocity and the same linear speed
  • X has half the angular velocity and half the linear speed of Y
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Every point on a rigid rotating disc sweeps the same angle per second; linear speed is $v = \omega r$, so it scales with distance from the centre.
#4multiple-choicefrom Q23inquiry 8.2
How did Thomson and Millikan each use fields to find a property of the electron?
  • ✓ Thomson deflected a cathode-ray beam with electric and magnetic fields to find the charge-to-mass ratio; Millikan balanced the electric force on charged oil drops against gravity to find the charge itself
  • Thomson measured the charge on oil drops; Millikan found the charge-to-mass ratio of cathode rays

💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Combining Millikan's charge with Thomson's charge-to-mass ratio gives the electron's mass.

#6multiple-choicefrom Q25ainquiry 5.3

  • ✓ Chemical potential energy of the fuel is converted into kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy of the satellite (both of which increase)
  • Kinetic energy is converted into chemical potential energy

#8multiple-choicefrom Q26binquiry 7.3
A student measures the voltage V needed to produce each of several light frequencies f, where $V = \dfrac{hf}{q_e}$. Rather than using a single data point, what is the best way to find Planck's constant from all the data?
  • ✓ Plot V against f, draw a line of best fit, and multiply its gradient by $q_e$ (since the gradient equals $h/q_e$)
  • Average the four voltage readings and divide by $q_e$
  • Use only the data point with the largest voltage, as it has the least percentage error
  • Take the difference between the largest and smallest voltage readings
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): A line of best fit uses every data point and averages out random errors; a single point carries all of its own error.

#13multiple-choicefrom Q32inquiry 8.1
A student stretches a straight elastic band with three evenly spaced marks so that the distance of every mark from the fixed end doubles. How well does this model the evidence for Hubble's expansion of the Universe?
  • ✓ It models it well: every mark moves away from every other, and marks that were further away move a greater distance in the same time (recession speed proportional to distance), which is Hubble's law
  • It does not model it at all, because the elastic band does not glow
  • It models it perfectly, including the fact that the Universe has a fixed edge
  • It models it well because the marks all move at the same speed

#14multiple-choicefrom Q33inquiry 7.3
Experiment A shines 400 nm light on a double slit and produces an interference pattern. Experiment B shines 400 nm light on metals: calcium (work function $4.60 \times 10^{-19}$ J) emits photoelectrons, nickel ($8.25 \times 10^{-19}$ J) does not. Which models of light do the two experiments support?
  • ✓ Experiment A supports the wave model (interference); Experiment B supports the particle model, since a single 400 nm photon carries about $5.0 \times 10^{-19}$ J, enough to free an electron from calcium but not from nickel
  • Both experiments support only the wave model of light
  • Both experiments support only the particle model of light
  • Experiment A supports the particle model; Experiment B supports the wave model
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Photon energy $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{400 \times 10^{-9}} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-19}$ J; compare it with each work function.
#15numeric-entryfrom Q34binquiry 5.1
A 3.0 kg mass is launched from a cliff edge. Its kinetic energy is 864 J at launch and 1393 J when it hits the ground. The horizontal component of its velocity throughout the flight is 13.76 m s$^{-1}$. Calculate the time of flight. Use $g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$.
4.8s
accepted within ±5% (relative)
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): From each KE, find the speed ($\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$), then the vertical velocity component ($v_y = \sqrt{v^2 - 13.76^2}$) at launch (upward) and at the ground (downward). The change in $v_y$ divided by $g$ is the time.

Plutonium-238 (238.0495 u) undergoes alpha decay to uranium-234 (234.0409 u) plus a helium-4 nucleus (4.0026 u). Calculate the energy released in one decay. Use 1 u = $1.661 \times 10^{-27}$ kg and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m s$^{-1}$.

#17numeric-entryfrom Q35binquiry 8.4
A generator initially contains $9.0 \times 10^{24}$ atoms of Pu-238 (half-life 87.7 years), each decay releasing $9.0 \times 10^{-13}$ J. Calculate the total energy produced by the generator during the first 10 years after launch.
620000000000.0J
accepted within ±6% (relative)
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Number of decays in 10 years = $N_0\left(1 - e^{-\lambda t}\right)$ with $\lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{87.7}$ per year; multiply by the energy per decay.
