A scientist has two unlabelled radiation sources, one an alpha emitter and one a beta emitter. Which pair of tests would identify which is which?
  • ✓ A penetration test (alpha is stopped by paper, beta passes paper but is stopped by a few mm of aluminium) and a deflection test in a magnetic field (both deflect, but beta far more, and in the opposite direction to alpha)
  • Weighing each source before and after a week
  • Measuring the colour of light each source emits
  • Checking which source feels warmer to the touch
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Alpha particles are heavy and +2 charged (low penetration, small deflection); beta particles are light and -1 charged (higher penetration, large deflection the other way).

  • ✓ The mass of A is three times the mass of B, because a satellite's orbital kinetic energy is $\dfrac{GMm}{2r}$, so equal kinetic energy with three times the radius needs three times the mass
  • A and B must have the same mass
  • The mass of A is one third the mass of B
  • The mass of A is nine times the mass of B

#6multiple-choicefrom Q25binquiry 7.3
Using the particle (photon) model of light, which features of the photoelectric results are explained?
  • ✓ There is a threshold frequency below which no electrons are emitted (each photon must carry at least the work function energy), and above it the stopping voltage rises linearly with frequency because $eV_{stop} = hf - \phi$
  • The stopping voltage depends only on how bright the light is, not its frequency
  • Photoelectrons are emitted at every frequency if you wait long enough
  • The current is zero at all frequencies
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): One photon gives its whole energy $hf$ to one electron; below $\phi$ that is not enough, and above it the surplus becomes kinetic energy.

#9multiple-choicefrom Q27inquiry 8.3
How does Schrodinger's model of the electron in an atom differ from the models of Rutherford and Bohr?
  • ✓ The electron is described by a wave function giving only the probability of finding it in each region (an orbital), with no definite path or orbit, and its energy levels emerge from solving a wave equation rather than being assumed
  • The electron orbits the nucleus in fixed circular paths at set radii
  • The electron is a stationary point charge at the centre of the atom

💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Rutherford and Bohr kept a definite orbit; Schrodinger replaced it with a probability distribution and no trajectory.

#11multiple-choicefrom Q29ainquiry 5.2
A mass on a string of length r moves in a vertical circle. At the highest point B the string tension is zero. Why is the speed there given by $v = \sqrt{rg}$?
  • ✓ With zero tension, gravity is the only force acting, so it alone provides the centripetal force: $mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, which rearranges to $v = \sqrt{rg}$
  • Because the mass is momentarily at rest at the top
  • Because the string does work on the mass at the top
  • Because the kinetic energy equals the potential energy at the top
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Zero tension means the weight is exactly the centripetal force needed to keep the mass on the circle.
#12multiple-choicefrom Q29binquiry 5.2
For the same mass on the vertical circle (speed $\sqrt{rg}$ at the top B), how does its speed at point A, level with the centre (a height r below B), compare?
  • ✓ It is faster: by conservation of energy $\tfrac{1}{2}v_A^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}v_B^2 + gr$, giving $v_A = \sqrt{3rg}$
  • It is the same, because the string length does not change
  • It is slower, because the string does negative work between B and A
  • It is exactly twice the speed at B
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Dropping a height r converts gravitational potential energy into extra kinetic energy: $v_A^2 = v_B^2 + 2gr = rg + 2gr$.

#18multiple-choicefrom Q35inquiry 6.3
A 50 g magnet is dropped inside an upright hollow copper pipe standing on a balance that reads 300 g. As the magnet falls, the balance reading rises to a steady 350 g before the magnet reaches the bottom. Explain the observations.
  • ✓ The falling magnet induces eddy currents in the pipe that oppose its motion (Lenz's law); the pipe pushes up on the magnet, so by Newton's third law the magnet pushes down on the pipe, raising the reading. Once the magnet reaches terminal velocity the retarding force equals its weight, so the reading is a constant 300 + 50 = 350 g
  • The magnet magnetises the copper, which becomes heavier
  • Air compressed below the magnet pushes down on the balance
  • The reading rises because the magnet speeds up and gains kinetic energy
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): At terminal velocity the whole weight of the magnet is transferred to the pipe through the magnetic interaction.
#19multiple-choicefrom Q36inquiry 5.1
A satellite of speed v splits into two equal-mass pieces at point Y. Piece $m_a$ leaves with speed 2v in the original direction. Using conservation of momentum, what happens to piece $m_b$ and its subsequent motion?
  • ✓ Momentum conservation ($mv + mv = m(2v) + mv_b$) gives $v_b = 0$: piece $m_b$ is momentarily at rest, so it no longer has orbital speed and falls towards Earth, while $m_a$ (now at 2v, above orbital speed) moves onto a larger, more elongated orbit or escapes
  • Both pieces continue in the original circular orbit at speed v
  • Piece $m_b$ also speeds up to 2v
  • Piece $m_b$ moves backwards along the orbit at speed v
💡 Tip (shown only if the student answers incorrectly): Total momentum before = total momentum after; with equal masses, if one piece doubles its speed the other must lose all of its forward speed.
